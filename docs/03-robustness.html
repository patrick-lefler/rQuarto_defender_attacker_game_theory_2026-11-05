Misreading the Attacker

Where Will the Attacker Strike? How each strategy copes when our read of the crew is wrong

All money is in $M. Losses are expected loss per targeted ransomware campaign, and the model assumes one campaign per year. All values are illustrative.

Why the textbook answer is fragile

The textbook equilibrium works by making the crew exactly indifferent across 5 targets. That razor’s edge is what makes the plan efficient, and also what makes it brittle: when the crew’s real payoffs differ from our estimates, the crew steps off the edge toward the target we left weakest.

Misjudged payoffs

We drew 3,000 versions of the crew. In each, every success payoff is multiplied by a random error (lognormal, sigma = 0.35). Every strategy faces the same draws.

How to read the plot. Each bar is one strategy, ordered from lowest average loss (top) to highest. The colored segments show the share of simulated crews that attack each asset, and the label is what that attack would cost us.

What the plot tells you. Robust funnels 68% of crews onto corporate IT, a cheap hit. The textbook plan scatters them across all six assets, and about one crew in 18 reaches the pipeline, a $46.5M loss.

NoteHow much each strategy relies on a perfect read

If our read of the crew is wrong, the robust strategy loses $1.6M more than planned, and the textbook strategy loses $10.3M more. The textbook plan’s efficiency rests almost entirely on accurate intelligence.

The robust equilibrium

The robust strategy assumes the crew mostly, but not always, picks the best target. It gives each target a chance of being hit that rises with how attractive it is to the crew, then minimizes our expected loss against those chances. A rationality parameter, \(\lambda\), controls how sharp the crew is.

We set \(\lambda =\) 2, chosen on a separate set of simulated crews so the choice is not tuned to the results we report. Optimizing at 2 gives the lowest average loss: $9.13M at 1, $9.03M at 2, $9.83M at 3 and $11.21M at 5.

NoteThe math behind the equilibrium

The crew attacks target \(t\) with probability

\[q_t(c) = \frac{\exp(\lambda U_a(t,c))}{\sum_s \exp(\lambda U_a(s,c))}.\]

We minimize \(f(c) = \sum_t q_t(c)\,\ell_t(c)\) over feasible coverage, with \(\ell_t\) our loss on target \(t\). The gradient is analytic and checked against finite differences. A multistart SLSQP search handles the nonconvexity.

How to read the plot. The horizontal axis shows how sharp the crew is: low values mean it picks targets almost at random, high values mean it always picks its best one. Each line is a strategy whose coverage is fixed. The vertical axis is our expected loss.

What the plot tells you. The robust strategy has the lowest loss once the crew is moderately sharp (2 is our base case; robust leads from 1 upward). For a nearly random crew, simpler plans do slightly better. The textbook equilibrium is worse than robust at every sharpness.

How to read the plot. Each line shows average loss by budget for one strategy when we misjudge the crew. The dotted line is our base budget.

What the plot tells you. Robust is lowest at nearly every budget. Extra money can hurt a simple plan: risk-ranked rises from $12.2M at $11.0M to $15.3M at $14.0M, and textbook from $15.3M at $9.0M to $17.0M at $11.0M, as new coverage pushes the crew toward a thin asset. At $5.5M a baseline edges ahead, though robust still beats the sharp crew it is built for. Above $16.5M every asset hits its cap.

Moving from $9.0M to $10.0M lowers the expected loss from misjudging the crew by $0.83M under robust and $1.55M under risk-ranked. Robust still leads at $10.0M: $8.2M against $12.3M. Across the budget grid from $5.0M to $20M, robust is lowest or tied at every budget except $5.5M.
